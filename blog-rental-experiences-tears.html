Rental Experiences, Tears

Adeline Quarshie · 7 February 2026 · 8 min read · Part 1 of a Series

What Our First Tenancy Taught Us

I have spent years advising clients on property acquisition, investment, and management in Ghana. I understand this market deeply. And yet, when we entered our first tenancy as a landlord — not as a consultant, but as the landlord — we discovered how much the market still had to teach us.

This is that story. It is not a comfortable one, but it is an honest one. And if you own property in Ghana, rent it, or are considering doing either, I believe it is one you need to read.

The Beginning: April 2025

In April 2025, Credence Real Estate & Asset Management formalised its first tenancy agreement as a landlord. The property was residential. The terms were structured around a four-month advance payment cycle plus a security deposit — an arrangement we considered reasonable, even generous, compared to the annual prepayment standard that many landlords in Ghana demand.

The tenant came across as credible. The relationship started on a footing of trust. We believed that goodwill, combined with a written tenancy agreement, was sufficient protection.

We were wrong — not about the goodwill, which was real — but about the assumption that a standard tenancy agreement, without precise specificity on use restrictions, payment triggers, and enforcement mechanisms, could protect us when things went wrong.

"A tenancy agreement is far more a legal relationship than it is a trust relationship." Adeline Quarshie, CEO — Credence Real Estate & Asset Management

The Complication: A Second Property

Early in the tenancy, discussions arose about a second property. There was a verbal understanding about the tenant occupying this second unit under a separate arrangement. The terms were discussed. Commitments were implied. Nothing was formalised in writing.

When the tenant's representatives later claimed entitlement to the second property under this verbal arrangement, we faced a challenge that should not have existed. The tenant had not made the required advance payments for that unit — payments that were explicitly the precondition for the arrangement proceeding. Without those payments, the arrangement had never legally crystallised.

And yet, because the conversation had happened — because words had been exchanged and expectations created — we found ourselves in a legal grey area that cost time, money, and energy to resolve.

The Discovery: Unauthorised Change of Use

By mid-2025, we discovered that our residential property had been converted into a podcast production studio — without our knowledge, without our consent, and in direct violation of the use restrictions in the tenancy agreement.

The residential property had become a commercial operation. This matters enormously. Residential and commercial tenancies carry different legal frameworks, different insurance requirements, different liability exposures, and fundamentally different conditions for use. What the tenant had done was not a minor adjustment — it was a fundamental change in the nature of the occupancy itself.

Police, Lawyers, and Escalation

An assault incident on the premises in July 2025 brought police involvement, and what had begun as a contractual tenancy dispute became entangled in a criminal investigation. We served appropriate notices. The tenant engaged legal representation. Their lawyers raised claims that expanded the dispute and shifted the matter from a simple tenancy issue into a more complex legal proceeding. We engaged our own legal counsel and began the formal process of recovering possession.

The process was slow. Ghana's legal and enforcement system, when it comes to tenancy disputes, is not designed for speed. Documentation that was absent from the outset created ambiguity. Verbal understandings that were never reduced to writing became contested facts. Every gap in our paperwork became a point of contention.

By January 2026 — nine months after the tenancy began, and after significant legal, financial, and operational cost — we recovered full possession of the property.

"Trust may start the conversation, but the law is what sustains — or ends — it." Adeline Quarshie

Seven Lessons for Property Owners in Ghana

The experience was not a failure of strategy — it was an education in specificity. These are the lessons we are carrying forward into every tenancy we manage, and the principles that now underpin our professional property management service.

Key Takeaways
01
Specificity of Use is Non-Negotiable

"Residential use only" is not enough. Your agreement must specify what activities are permitted, which are prohibited, and what the consequences of violation are — including immediate notice to vacate.

02
No Advance Payment Means No Agreement

Any verbal arrangement about a property must be tied to a clear payment trigger. If the payment is not made, the arrangement does not exist — and that must be explicit in writing.

03
Document Every Conversation

Every verbal discussion about the property — including intentions to sell, extend, or modify terms — must be followed by a written record, even if only a message confirmed by both parties.

04
Structure the Security Deposit Precisely

The deposit amount, the conditions under which deductions may be made, and the return timeline must all be explicit. Vague deposit clauses always favour the tenant in a dispute.

05
Criminal Involvement Multiplies Complexity

Once a criminal matter is introduced into a tenancy dispute, the complexity multiplies significantly. Your agreement must define what constitutes a material breach warranting immediate termination.

06
Know Your Enforcement Timeline

In Ghana, recovering possession through legal channels takes time. Landlords must be financially and operationally prepared for the process to run longer than expected. Budget for it.

07
Professional Management is Protection, Not a Cost

This experience reinforced what we tell our clients: professional property management — with proper documentation, structured payment systems, and experienced oversight — is not an expense. It is how you preserve the value of your investment.

What Comes Next

This is the first in a series of articles in which I will share the full details of this experience — the legal mechanisms we used, the documentation we wish we had prepared, and the practical implications for property owners and diaspora investors across Ghana.

Ghana's property market is growing. Its legal frameworks are evolving. Property owners who are informed, disciplined, and properly represented will be those who build and preserve generational wealth from their investments.

If you own property in Ghana and are considering renting it — or if you are currently a landlord navigating a difficult tenancy — I invite you to speak with our team at Credence Real Estate & Asset Management. Our professional property management service is designed to protect your investment from precisely these situations.
